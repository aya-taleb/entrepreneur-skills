# Interview guide

The rules behind a good discovery talk, with examples. The idea of asking about past
behavior instead of opinions comes from Rob Fitzpatrick's book "The Mom Test". This is
our own short version.

## The three rules

1. **Talk about their life, not your idea.** Ask what happened, what they did, what it
   cost. People are kind about ideas and honest about their week.
2. **Ask about the past, not the future.** "Would you use...?" gets a polite yes.
   "When did this last happen?" gets a real story.
3. **Listen more than you talk.** If you pitched, the interview stopped being research.

## Questions that work

- "Tell me about the last time you <did the job>. What happened?"
- "What was the hardest part?"
- "What did you do about it?" / "What do you use for that today?"
- "How much time or money does that take? Who pays?"
- "What have you tried that didn't work? Why did you stop?"
- "Who else deals with this? Could you introduce me?"
- "Is there anything I should have asked?"

## Questions that mislead

| don't ask | why | ask instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Would you use an app that...? | Everyone says yes to be nice | When did you last try to solve this? |
| How much would you pay for...? (alone) | Guesses about the future | What do you pay for it now? |
| Do you think this is a good idea? | Asks for a compliment | What's the hardest part of <job>? |
| Wouldn't it be great if...? | Leading | What would you change about how you do it? |

The only future-facing questions worth asking come at the very end, as a commitment
test: "We're starting a pilot in November. Want in?" A real yes costs them something.

## Reading the signal

| what they did | signal |
| --- | --- |
| Paid, pre-ordered, signed a letter of intent | committed, strong |
| Gave real time (a pilot, a second meeting), or an intro to a peer or their boss | committed |
| Said "love it", "keep me posted", "send me a link" | interested, weak |
| Changed the subject, couldn't recall the last time it happened | none |

Compliments, hypothetical promises and "I'd definitely use that" are not evidence. Log
them as interested, and don't count them as demand.

## Price questions (optional, end of the talk)

Only after you understand what they do today. Four questions, in this order:

1. At what price would this be so cheap you'd doubt the quality? (too_cheap)
2. At what price would it feel like a bargain? (cheap)
3. At what price would it start to feel expensive, but you'd still think about it? (expensive)
4. At what price would it be too expensive to consider? (too_expensive)

These are the Van Westendorp questions. With 20 or more answers from one segment, a price
tool can draw an acceptable range. With fewer, read them one by one, as stories.

## Who to talk to

- People who had the problem recently, in the segment the business file names.
- Not friends and family, unless they truly are the buyer. They're too kind.
- Start where the buyers already gather: the founder's own network, local business
  groups, online communities where the buyers ask questions, past customers of similar
  products. Ask; don't scrape. Messaging people at scale through a platform's automation
  breaks its rules and burns the founder's name.
- 5 to 10 talks in one narrow segment beat 30 spread across many.

## Asking for the talk

Short, honest, no pitch. Say who you are, what you're learning about, how long it takes,
and that you're not selling. Offer to share what you learn. The founder sends it.
